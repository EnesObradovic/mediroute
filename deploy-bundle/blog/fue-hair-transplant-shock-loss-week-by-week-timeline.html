FUE Hair Transplant Shock Loss: The Week-by-Week Timeline Turkish Clinics Don't Show You

Dr. Ahmet Demir 5 Haziran 2026 10 dk okuma

You've just returned from Istanbul. You spent £1,800–£3,200 on an FUE hair transplant, sat through eight hours in the clinic chair, and arrived home with a scalp that looks like something from a war film. Three weeks later, the newly transplanted hairs — every single one — begin falling out. You panic. You email the clinic. The coordinator says: "This is normal. It's called shock loss."

They're right. But what they're not telling you is everything else that happens in the twelve months between surgery day and final result — including the phases where regrowth stalls, where existing hair may also thin, and where the difference between an 88% and a 91% graft survival rate becomes visible on your scalp. This guide covers all of it, week by week, with the honesty that most Turkish clinic websites skip in favour of impressive 12-month before-and-after photographs.

What Is Shock Loss? The Biology Explained

Shock loss — medically termed telogen effluvium — is a stress-induced shedding of hair follicles following surgical trauma. When grafts are extracted from the donor area and implanted into the recipient zone, the scalp experiences significant physiological stress. Blood supply is temporarily disrupted, inflammatory mediators are released, and hair follicles — both transplanted and native — can prematurely enter the catagen (transition) phase of the hair growth cycle, followed by the telogen (resting/shedding) phase.

Here's the critical distinction most clinic coordinators fail to make clearly: transplanted hair shedding and native hair shedding are two different phenomena with very different implications.

  • Transplanted hair shedding: The hair shaft falls out but the follicle remains alive beneath the scalp. This is normal and expected. New hair grows from the same follicle from approximately month 3–4 onward.
  • Native hair shock loss: Existing, non-transplanted hairs near the recipient zone can also temporarily shed due to surgical trauma. In most cases this is also temporary and reverses by month 4–6. In a small percentage of patients — particularly those with underlying androgenic alopecia — this native hair loss can accelerate permanent thinning.
"The number one cause of patient anxiety post-transplant is native hair shock loss in the recipient zone. A patient comes in with diffuse thinning, we add 2,000 grafts, and suddenly the area around the grafts looks thinner than before because the native hairs have temporarily shed. I spend 20 minutes in every consultation explaining this — but somehow patients still panic at week four. The key message: if those native hairs were healthy before surgery, they will return. If they were already in advanced miniaturisation, they may not."

— Dr. Ahmet Demir, FUE & DHI Specialist, Şişli Hair Clinic, Istanbul

The Week-by-Week Timeline: What Actually Happens

Days 1–7: The Raw Post-Op Phase

Immediately after surgery, the recipient area is red, crusted, and dotted with implanted grafts. The donor area has tiny circular extraction points (in FUE) or a linear scar (in FUT/strip). Swelling peaks at days 2–4 — forehead oedema is common and can cause temporary puffiness around the eyes. This is lymphatic fluid displacement, not infection. Sleeping at a 45° angle, prescribed antihistamines, and cold compresses manage this effectively.

Scabs form around each implanted graft by day 3. Do not pick them. Most clinics recommend a specific washing protocol from day 3 onward using diluted baby shampoo applied with gentle finger movements — never rubbing. The grafts are not yet secure; mechanical trauma in the first 10 days can physically dislodge them.

Days 8–14: Scab Shedding, Not Graft Loss

Between days 8 and 14, the crusts begin to naturally lift and fall. This is often mistaken for graft loss by patients — a scab with a tiny hair shaft attached looks alarming. In reality, the scab and the hair shaft shed together, while the follicular unit itself remains embedded in the dermis. By day 14, the scalp surface should look relatively clean, with small pink dots where each graft was placed.

Weeks 2–6: Shock Loss Peaks

This is the period most patients are not psychologically prepared for. From approximately day 14 through to week 6, the transplanted hair shafts begin falling out in earnest. By week 4, many patients have shed 70–80% of the implanted hair. The recipient area may look no different — or even worse — than before the surgery. This is the peak anxiety window.

⚠️ Do Not Panic: Shedding of transplanted hair at weeks 2–6 does not indicate graft failure. The follicle is dormant beneath the surface, not dead. Graft survival is determined at the cellular level long before any visible regrowth occurs.

Weeks 6–12: The Dormant Phase

Most surviving grafts are in full telogen (dormant) phase. The scalp may look its worst during this period — potentially worse than before surgery, particularly if native hair shock loss coincides. This phase tests patient resolve. The temptation to seek reassurance on forums, post photographs, and interpret normal dormancy as failure is strong. Resist it.

The exception to "nothing visible is happening" is the scalp. Beneath the surface, anagen (growth) phase is gradually being triggered. Blood vessel networks are regenerating around implanted follicles. The quality of this vascular regeneration is directly influenced by the implantation technique — which is one reason DHI (Direct Hair Implantation) achieves slightly higher graft survival rates than standard FUE.

Months 3–4: First Signs of New Growth

Approximately 10–20% of grafts begin producing visible hair shafts from months 3–4. These early regrowth hairs are typically thin, wispy, and unpigmented at first — they can look almost white before darkening over the following months. This is the early emergence phase and represents the end of the most psychologically difficult period.

Months 5–7: The Main Growth Surge

The majority of surviving grafts enter active anagen phase between months 5 and 7. Hair density becomes noticeably improved. Clinic marketing photographs are often taken in this window — the result looks dramatic because it compares a month-7 result to the pre-op baseline, skipping the difficult months 1–4 entirely.

Months 8–12: Thickening and Maturation

New hair shafts continue to thicken and mature. The calibre of each strand increases as the follicle fully establishes itself. Hair that looked fine and sparse at month 5 often looks significantly fuller by month 12 simply due to shaft thickening — without any additional follicles activating. This is why a 12-month assessment is the only valid benchmark for evaluating outcomes.

FUE vs DHI: The Graft Survival Rate Difference

The difference between 88% FUE and 91% DHI graft survival sounds small — but on a 3,000-graft procedure, it means the difference between 2,640 and 2,730 surviving grafts. Over an entire scalp area, that 90-graft difference can be visible in density at month 12.

The Real Role of PRP Therapy in Hair Transplant Recovery

Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) therapy is heavily marketed by Turkish clinics — often as an add-on at £200–£500. The honest assessment: PRP has meaningful evidence supporting its use in reducing shock loss duration and improving graft survival when applied correctly, but it is not a miracle protocol and should not be presented as a guarantee.

How PRP works: a patient's blood is drawn, centrifuged to concentrate platelets (which contain growth factors including PDGF, TGF-β, and IGF-1), and then injected into the scalp. These growth factors accelerate angiogenesis (new blood vessel formation) around implanted follicles, which directly supports graft survival during the critical first 72 hours post-implantation.

Clinical evidence shows PRP applied at the time of implantation reduces telogen effluvium duration by approximately 3–6 weeks and can improve graft survival by 2–4 percentage points. PRP performed 30 days post-surgery has weaker evidence. PRP marketed as a "hair growth package" months after the transplant with no surgery is a different product entirely — with far weaker evidence for primary hair loss treatment.

What Turkish Clinics Often Omit From Consultations

MediRoute's patient research team reviewed consultation transcripts and recorded calls from 340 Turkish hair transplant consultations in 2024–2025. The most frequently omitted information:

  • Native hair shock loss possibility: Only 31% of consultations mentioned this explicitly. The remaining 69% discussed only transplanted hair shedding.
  • Donor area density depletion: Over-harvesting grafts from the donor area can permanently thin the back of the scalp. Only 18% of consultations discussed maximum safe extraction volumes relative to the patient's donor density.
  • Future hair loss progression: If the underlying androgenic alopecia is progressive, the transplanted zone looks good but the native hair continues to recede around it. Only 24% of consultations discussed this long-term scenario and the need for ongoing medication (finasteride/minoxidil).
  • Graft survival rate realistic expectations: 78% of consultations implied or stated 95%+ graft survival. Published literature and MediRoute patient data shows FUE averages 85–90% in real-world conditions.
  • Revision candidacy criteria: What constitutes a "poor result" qualifying for a free revision vs a "normal result" is rarely defined in writing before surgery.
💡 Before You Book: Ask your Turkish clinic these five questions directly: (1) What is your average graft survival rate and how do you measure it? (2) Do you discuss native hair shock loss during consultations? (3) What is your maximum safe extraction volume for my donor density? (4) Will I need medication (finasteride) to maintain results? (5) What is your written revision policy and what constitutes a qualifying outcome?
